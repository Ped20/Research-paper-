## 4. The predictive gap: does early-stage screening forecast field performance?

Early-stage screening with PEG is justified by an assumption that is rarely stated: that the genotypes it ranks as tolerant at germination or the seedling stage will also perform better under drought at the reproductive stage — the stage at which yield is actually determined. This section tests that assumption against the studies that have examined it directly.

**The literature divides, and it is not divided by crop.**

### 4.1 Evidence that early-stage ranking does not transfer

**Wheat — trait correlations with yield are absent.** A population of 146 F₉ recombinant inbred lines derived from a cross between a seedling-drought-tolerant and a seedling-drought-susceptible cultivar was scored for seedling tolerance, survival and recovery indices, then grown for grain yield in two low-rainfall environments across two seasons ✅. The conclusion is stark: **no significant correlations were found between seedling traits and grain yield in any environment**. Of the genotypes identified as most drought-tolerant at the seedling stage, only one combined that with high yield under drought. The authors' own recommendation is that breeding for seedling tolerance *and* for yield should be studied separately under controlled and field conditions — that is, they do not treat the seedling screen as a proxy ✅.

**The same study identified a deeper problem.** Analyses of tolerance traits (time to wilting, leaf wilting score) and recovery traits (days to regrowth, regrowth biomass, survival rate) found them to be **weakly correlated or uncorrelated**, and the quantitative trait loci underpinning them were **entirely different**, except for a single pleiotropic locus ✅. Tolerance and recovery at the same developmental stage are therefore genetically distinct — a finding that undercuts the common practice of treating a single composite index as a general measure of "drought tolerance."

**Barley — a negative correlation, and an implausible result.** A 164-line spring barley population was screened under PEG at germination and seedling stages, then subjected to short-term drought at heading, with grain yield measured ✅. Germination rate under PEG correlated **negatively** with seed yield under drought (r = −0.25), a result the authors describe as "unexpected and warrants further investigation" ✅. Germination percentage also correlated negatively with root:shoot fresh weight under PEG (r = −0.37) ✅. Most tellingly, **some genotypes performed better under PEG than under control** for some traits — biologically incoherent for a genuine water-deficit treatment, and direct evidence that PEG introduces effects beyond water limitation ✅.

The study was designed specifically to test "the reliability of proxy methods in identifying drought tolerant lines" ✅. Nine lines germinated at 100% under PEG; of those, **three maintained root length and only two showed no yield penalty under heading-stage drought** ✅. A roughly 22% transfer rate from screening to field, within a purpose-built experiment.

**Wheat — germination does not predict seedling stage.** A study that scored the same genotypes for germination traits under PEG and for a seedling-stage drought tolerance index found **little to no correlation between the two stages**, citing two further studies reaching the same conclusion, and concluded that testing the same genotypes across growth stages is essential ✅. This is the shortest interval in the developmental sequence, and the association still fails.

### 4.2 Evidence that early-stage ranking does transfer

**Wheat — seedling traits do correlate with yield at moderate stress.** A diallel analysis found grain yield per spike significantly correlated with root length (r = 0.41, P < 0.05) and seedling dry weight (r = 0.46, P < 0.05) at 15% PEG ✅. Under 20% PEG the correlations weakened and root:shoot ratio became non-significant ✅. Notably, the relationship was **concentration-dependent** — the screen worked at a moderate stress level and degraded at a severe one, which bears directly on the choice of screening concentration.

**Barley — a consistency claim.** A GWAS of 198 genotypes states that "drought tolerance in barley is consistent when determined at the three important growth stages: the germination and seedling stage, the vegetative growth stage, and the flowering and yield stages" ✅. This directly contradicts the 164-line barley study above. Both are recent, both use large populations, both use PEG — and they disagree.

**Tomato — the Solanaceae exception.** The single Solanaceae study that connects stages used *Solanum pennellii* introgression lines that had already been ranked for drought tolerance at germination and seedling stages, then evaluated them through vegetative and reproductive stages under two water regimes ✅. Two lines — **IL 1-4-18 and IL 1-2** — had been ranked tolerant at germination/seedling and were again tolerant at vegetative/reproductive stages, and the most tolerant line at the early stages was also the most tolerant at the later ones ✅. Candidate genes identified in the introgressed segments included *AHG2*, *PRXIIF*, *SAP5*, *PRXQ*, *CFS1*, *LCD*, *CCD1* and *SCS* ✅.

**Potato — phenotypic biomarkers hold across trials.** Across three trials on 20 tetraploid potato lines, growth-curve parameters — the turning point of leaf area index and of projected leaf area — correlated with drought tolerance independently of the treatment in which they were measured ✅. This is a *vegetative-to-yield* transfer rather than germination-to-yield, but it demonstrates that some early-measured traits do carry predictive signal.

### 4.3 Why the results conflict: four candidate explanations

The contradiction is not random. At least four design and biological factors plausibly drive it, and stating them converts a confusing literature into an actionable one.

**First, internal versus cross-stage concordance are different questions.** Several studies reporting "strong correlations" are correlating traits *within* a single stage or treatment — for example, germination percentage with germination rate, or root with shoot biomass ✅. These are near-tautological relationships between co-measured growth traits. They are routinely reported in the same correlation matrices as cross-stage relationships, which makes a study appear to demonstrate transfer when it has only demonstrated internal consistency. **This is the single most common source of apparent support for predictive validity, and it should be separated in every correlation analysis.**

**Second, trait-level correlation and genotype-level overlap diverge.** In the wheat study above, *no* seedling trait correlated with grain yield ✅ — yet individual genotypes were identified that were tolerant at the seedling stage and high-yielding in the field ✅. A screening programme does not need a significant trait correlation; it needs to retain the right genotypes. Reviews that dismiss early screening on the basis of absent trait correlations may be applying the wrong test. **The correct metric is the concordance rate among selected genotypes, and almost nobody reports it.**

**Third, PEG concentration determines whether the screen discriminates.** The diallel study's positive correlations at 15% PEG became non-significant at 20% ✅. Severe stress can drive most genotypes to the floor of the measurable range, destroying resolution. Combined with the finding that most tomato genotypes fail to germinate at all above −0.35 MPa (§2), this argues against a fixed concentration applied to all genotypes — and in favour of the genotype-specific LC50 approach used in the tomato callus work ✅.

**Fourth, the traits being screened may not be the traits that matter.** Tolerance and recovery at the same stage are genetically distinct ✅; drought tolerance is stage-specific within *Capsicum* itself, with all three cultivated species more susceptible at the vegetative stage than at flowering or fruiting ✅. If tolerance at different stages is controlled by largely non-overlapping loci, then a single-stage screen cannot be a general proxy — and the search for one is misdirected.

### 4.4 What this means for practice

Three operational conclusions follow.

**Early-stage screening should be described as what it is.** It ranks genotypes for tolerance *at that stage*. It is not a proxy for field drought tolerance, and the literature does not support presenting it as one. Papers should stop calling the PEG treatment "drought" without qualification (§2).

**The confirmation rate should be reported, not assumed.** Where a screen feeds a breeding programme, the fraction of selected genotypes that prove superior under field drought is the quantity that matters. It is currently almost never reported. This is a reporting gap, and closing it is cheap: it requires only that field evaluation of screened material is published alongside the screen.

**The unresolved cases should be resolved deliberately.** Barley has two large, recent studies reaching opposite conclusions ✅✅. That is a tractable dispute: the difference may lie in population, PEG concentration, stress timing, or the traits scored. It should be settled by analysing both datasets together rather than by one more independent screen. **This is the review's most concrete proposal for future work.**

## Reviewer-facing notes

1. **This section is now the review's strongest.** A genuinely contradictory literature, explained by four identifiable design factors, with a concrete proposal to resolve it — that is R4–R5 territory.
2. **The single best original point is §4.3, first explanation.** "Internal concordance is being reported as cross-stage transfer" is a methodological criticism nobody appears to have made explicitly for this literature. It explains the contradiction better than any biological account.
3. **The second best is the concordance-rate point.** If reviews reject early screening for absent trait correlations, they may be applying the wrong test. Say this carefully — it is a *methodological* claim, and it needs the wheat example to support it.
4. **Verify the barley contradiction carefully before publishing it.** Two studies from the same crop reaching opposite conclusions is a strong claim. Read both in full; confirm the contradiction is real and not an artefact of different trait definitions.
5. **Do not overclaim in either direction.** The honest position is: the evidence is mixed, the design factors are identifiable, and the question is unresolved in *Capsicum* specifically. That last point is the gap the review identifies.

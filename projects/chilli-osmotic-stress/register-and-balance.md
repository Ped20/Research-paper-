# Register and Persuasiveness — How to Write a Critical Review the Field Accepts

**Purpose:** This document exists because our draft currently contains **seven withdrawn or reversed claims across three sections**. That is a high correction rate, and it reveals something important about how this review must be written.

**The problem it creates:** a review that repeatedly overturns conventional wisdom reads as *contrarian*. Contrarian reviews do not get cited — they get argued with, or rejected. Accuracy is necessary but not sufficient. **The review must be convincing to the field it critiques.**

---

## 1. Why the corrections happened — and what it tells us

The seven corrections were not random. They clustered:

| Section | Claim | What happened |
| --- | --- | --- |
| §5 | *Capsicum* lacks validated drought loci | Contradicted by a 2026 GWAS+QTL study |
| §5 | No study connects developmental strata | Contradicted by nine connecting studies |
| §4 | Predictive link is negative | Actually contradictory — 4 support, 4 contradict |
| §2 | PEG impurities cause artefacts | **Tested and rejected in 1970** |
| §2 | PEG detergent effects cause toxicity | **Tested and rejected in 1970** |
| §2 | PEG does not enter plant tissues | Contradicted by direct measurement |
| §2 | "Drought" is an appropriate label | Correct, but had to become a finding rather than an assumption |

**The pattern: conventional wisdom in this field is repeated more than it is checked.** That is a genuine, publishable observation — and it is also a warning. If the review asserts something that a referee knows to have been tested and rejected, the review is finished.

### The strategic reading

A correction rate this high is *good material*, badly framed. The honest framing is not "everyone is wrong." It is:

> **Several widely repeated methodological assumptions in this literature were tested decades ago and not supported, yet the tests are rarely cited. That is a citation problem, not a competence problem — and it is fixable.**

That framing is defensible, constructive, and sympathetic to the field. It is also true.

## 2. The balance rule — every criticism needs four things

A review earns the right to criticise by doing four things each time. Our drafts do some of these; the rule should be applied consistently.

| Element | Function | Where ours stands |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Acknowledge the achievement** | Shows the criticism is not a dismissal | §3.3 added; §2 and §4 need explicit acknowledgement paragraphs |
| **2. Scope the criticism precisely** | Prevents overbroad claims a referee can refute | §3.4 qualification paragraph does this well; §2.6 does it; §4.1 does not yet |
| **3. Explain why the error is understandable** | Removes implied incompetence | Largely missing — **this is the biggest gap** |
| **4. Give a cheap fix** | Makes the review constructive | Strong throughout — LC50, GLMM, shared panels, index pairs |

**Element 3 is what we are missing, and it is the single highest-value addition.** Reviewers defend authors. If the review explains *why* a reasonable researcher made each choice, the criticism becomes collaborative rather than adversarial.

### Worked examples of element 3

| Criticism | The "why it's understandable" version |
| --- | --- |
| "The field uses ANOVA on binomial germination data" | *"ANOVA has a long history of accepted use in seed science, and for many crops with high germination uniformity the assumptions are met in practice. The conditions that drought screening creates — means driven toward 0 and 100% — are precisely those where the method's assumptions fail, and this is a consequence of the experimental design rather than of analytical carelessness."* |
| "Papers cite the non-penetration of PEG without evidence" | *"The non-penetration assumption was reasonable when first advanced and has been reproduced in the introductions of hundreds of papers since. The original measurements predate the modern literature cycle by fifty years and are effectively outside the citation reach of authors writing today."* |
| "Studies apply a fixed PEG concentration to all genotypes" | *"Fixed concentrations are the only practical choice when screening panels are large and prior information about individual genotypes is absent. The LC50 approach requires a pre-experiment that many screening programmes are not resourced to run."* |

Each version makes the same point while giving the author a reason. That is the difference between a review the field cites and one it resents.

## 3. The five moves that make a critical review persuasive

### Move 1 — Lead with what the field has accomplished

§3.3 is the model. Before any critical section, state what the existing work achieved. The reader should never doubt that the reviewer understands and values the literature.

**Apply this to §4.** It currently opens with evidence against predictive validity. It should open with the observation that the question exists *because* screening is worth validating — the field invested in the screens because they produce useful outputs.

### Move 2 — Criticise the method, never the authors

Say "the literature reports nominal concentrations without measurement" rather than "authors fail to measure." Passive, systemic framing. Reviewers notice.

### Move 3 — Give the constructive alternative before the criticism lands

The LC50, GLMM, index-pair and shared-panel recommendations are already in the draft. In several places they appear *after* the criticism. Moving them adjacent — criticism, then immediately the fix — changes the reading experience entirely.

### Move 4 — Quantify honestly, and separate what was counted from what was read

The 70% ANOVA / 87.6% arcsine / 17.4% pseudoreplication figures come from a systematic review of 429 studies ✅. **They must be attributed, never presented as our own count.** Attributing them strengthens the argument; the alternative would be misrepresentation and would be caught.

### Move 5 — State the counter-evidence prominently

§2.4 reports a 1973 study that *endorses* PEG for germination work. §4.2 reports four studies supporting predictive transfer. This is not weakness — it is what distinguishes a review from an advocacy piece, and it is why a referee will trust the other conclusions.

## 4. Framing options — pick one and be consistent

The review's stance should be chosen deliberately and held throughout. Three viable framings:

| Framing | Tone | Risk | Best if |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **A. Constructive correction** — "the literature is valuable; several assumptions need revisiting" | Collaborative | May read as cautious | **Recommended.** Safest for a first review, and accurate |
| **B. Methodological audit** — "here is what the methods can and cannot support" | Neutral, technical | Understates the thesis | If targeting a methods-focused venue |
| **C. Provocation** — "the field has been measuring the wrong thing" | High impact | Needs overwhelming evidence; invites hostile review | Not recommended with seven corrections already |

**Choose A.** With a correction rate of seven and a first-time review team, framing C would be attacked on tone before substance. Framing A makes the same substantive points and is much harder to reject.

### Framing A in one sentence, for the abstract and Introduction

> Early-stage osmotic screening has produced a substantial body of tolerant germplasm across the Solanaceae, but the methodological assumptions underpinning its interpretation have been less examined than its outputs; this review assesses what those methods can support and proposes a standard for making them cumulative.

That sentence commits to nothing the evidence does not support, credits the field, and states the contribution.

## 5. Title and abstract register

Titles promising a demolition will be read with hostility. Compare:

| ❌ Hostile | ✅ Constructive |
| --- | --- |
| "Why PEG screening fails to identify drought-tolerant genotypes" | "Osmotic screening in the Solanaceae: assumptions, evidence, and a standard for cumulative screening" |
| "The drought tolerance mirage" | "From Petri dish to field: assessing what early-stage osmotic screening can support" |

The recommended title from the outline — *Screening for drought tolerance in* Capsicum *and the Solanaceae: methodological artefacts, an untested predictive link, and a cost-tiered path to markers* — sits between the two. **"Methodological artefacts" is defensible; "a cost-tiered path" is the constructive promise.** Keep it, but consider softening "untested" to "unresolved," which matches the corrected §4 finding more accurately and reads less accusatory.

> **Recommended revision:** *Screening for drought tolerance in* Capsicum *and the Solanaceae: methodological assumptions, an unresolved predictive link, and a cost-tiered path to markers*

---

## 6. Checklist — run before submission

- [ ] Every critical section opens with an achievement or a concession
- [ ] Every criticism is scoped to a method, never to authors
- [ ] Every criticism includes a "why this choice was reasonable" sentence
- [ ] Every criticism is followed immediately by a feasible fix
- [ ] Counted figures are attributed to their sources, not presented as ours
- [ ] Counter-evidence is reported in the same section as the criticism, not buried
- [ ] Soft claims are flagged as flags: "may," "in the studies we identified," "appears"
- [ ] No claim asserts a universal negative without a stated search
- [ ] The thesis is stated as a gap to close, not a failing to expose
- [ ] Every numerical claim in the abstract appears in a table or figure

---

## 7. The one-sentence test

Read the abstract as a sceptical referee whose own paper is in the reference list.

**If the reaction is "these people think I've been doing it wrong" — rewrite.**
**If the reaction is "this is fair, and I should check whether my data were analysed correctly" — submit.**

The second reaction is what produces citations, and citations are the only durable measure of whether a review convinced the field.

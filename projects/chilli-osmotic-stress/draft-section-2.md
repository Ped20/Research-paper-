## 2. Osmotic stress simulation: chemistry, contested assumptions, and documented artefacts

Every germination- or seedling-stage drought screen in the Solanaceae literature rests on the same substitution: water deficit is replaced by an osmoticum, almost always polyethylene glycol of molecular weight 6000 or 8000, applied at a nominal percentage. This section examines that substitution. It is placed before the results of those screens because the limitations of the treatment determine what the screens can and cannot show.

### 2.1 PEG is not one substance: the molecular-weight problem

"PEG" describes a polymer series, not a reagent. Molecular weights from 200 to 20,000 are in use, and the literature treats them as interchangeable. They are not.

Osmotic pressure at equal mass concentration differs systematically with molecular weight: measurements across PEGs of *M*ᵣ 200–10,000 showed that **low molecular weight PEGs generate higher osmotic pressure than higher molecular weight PEGs at the same mass concentration** ✅. The ratio of concentration to molecular weight governs the effect, so a 15% (w/v) solution of PEG 400 and of PEG 6000 are not equivalent treatments — yet screening papers routinely state a percentage without discussing weight.

Molecular weight also determines **where** the osmotic stress acts. Comparing PEG 4000 and PEG 6000 in common bean root tips, PEG 4000 was found to act mainly in the cytoplasm while PEG 6000 acted additionally in the apoplast ✅. The two treatments therefore impose stress at different cellular sites, and a study switching between them has changed its experiment.

Even the choice among high molecular weights matters for the plant. In a study on **pepper (*Capsicum annuum* L.) itself**, plants were grown in nutrient solutions at −3.0 or −5.0 bar using PEG 400, 600, 1000, 1540 and 4000 ✅. PEG of 1000 and 1540 was judged most satisfactory as an osmoticum; PEG accumulated in plant tissue in inverse proportion to its molecular weight; and **the major proportion of absorbed PEG 4000 was retained in the roots rather than translocated** ✅. This is a direct demonstration in the review's focal crop that the osmoticum is not a passive background variable.

**Practical consequence:** papers should report PEG molecular weight, supplier and batch. Most do not.

### 2.2 Concentration is not osmotic potential — for three separate reasons

Screening studies typically report a percentage or a nominal figure in MPa. Three independent factors break the equivalence.

**First, PEG solutions are not colligative.** PEG does not behave as an ideal solute. Measurements in solution found that **PEG produces a greater osmotic effect than can be accounted for by the number of molecules present**, an effect attributed to water sequestration and non-colligative behaviour, with the discrepancy correlating with polymer mass ✅. A separate analysis noted that PEG behaviour is "related to structural changes in the PEG polymer" rather than behaving as most salts and sugars do ✅. Nominal conversion tables therefore carry an error of unstated magnitude.

**Second, osmotic potential is temperature-dependent.** Michel and Kaufmann's empirical equation for PEG-6000 — still the standard reference ✅ — expresses ψs as curvilinearly related to concentration and **linearly increasing with temperature, over a range of only 15–35 °C** ✅. A screening experiment run at 20 °C and one at 30 °C therefore apply different stresses at the same stated concentration. Incubator temperature is a treatment variable.

**Third, measurement method changes the answer.** Michel and Kaufmann explicitly warned that **thermocouple psychrometer readings are more negative than vapour pressure osmometer readings**, judging the psychrometer closer to correct for bulk solutions ✅. Better-characterised PEG work has since noted that differences between osmometer and psychrometer, and between osmometer types, are large enough to matter ✅. "Osmotic potential measured" is therefore not a complete methods statement — the instrument must be specified.

**Practical consequence:** measuring osmotic potential is better than assuming it, but measurement is itself method-dependent. Report the instrument. Maintain and report constant temperature.

### 2.3 The penetration question: an assumption that does not hold as stated

The standard justification for PEG is that, unlike mannitol or sucrose, it is excluded from plant tissues — so the stress it imposes is purely osmotic, with no confounding uptake. This is the argument that appears in the introduction of most PEG screening papers, and it is why PEG displaced mannitol. The classic literature does not support it in the form it is usually stated.

Direct measurement in maize, bean, cotton and tulip tree found that **PEG of 1000 molecular weight and above entered plants, at roughly 1 mg per gram leaf fresh weight per week**, with entry substantially increased by mechanically damaged roots or by low water potentials — precisely the conditions of a drought experiment ✅. Autoradiography localised absorbed PEG 4000 initially at leaf margins, then through the mesophyll ✅.

In **pepper**, absorbed PEG was distributed differently by molecular weight: for all but PEG 4000 there was more PEG in leaves than roots, and once leaf concentration reached 1–2 mg/mL, further absorbed material was transferred to the leaves ✅.

Two qualifications must be stated fairly, because the picture is not one-sided:

- **Entry is slow for high molecular weights.** At approximately 1 mg/g FW/week, uptake over a 7–10 day germination assay is small in absolute terms. The claim is not that PEG 6000 floods the tissue.
- **Modern convention assumes exclusion, and much of the applied literature asserts it.** Statements that PEG "does not enter plant tissues" are common ✅. But an assertion repeated in introductions is not a measurement, and the measurements that exist point the other way, at least for lower molecular weights and stressed or damaged roots.

**What follows for interpretation.** If PEG can enter the tissue, then a treatment described as purely osmotic may carry a solute-loading component. The magnitude depends on molecular weight, duration, root integrity and stress intensity — all of which vary across the studies this review catalogues. The correct conclusion is not that PEG screens are invalid; it is that **the mechanism of action is less well characterised than the standard justification implies**, and that the justification should be stated as an assumption rather than a fact.

This also means **the choice of PEG over mannitol is less decisive than it is presented as being.** The critique of mannitol — that it is taken up and metabolised — is correct, but the contrast with PEG is one of degree, not of kind. The most recent direct comparison of the two in *Cucurbita* landraces concluded that **mannitol simulated osmotic stress better than PEG for most attributes and concentrations**, while also finding PEG more effective as a discriminator for some biochemical traits ✅ — the opposite of the conventional ranking. A durum wheat study reached the opposite conclusion again, finding PEG superior and noting that **mannitol's effects were reversible**, consistent with uptake ✅.

**The comparative ranking of osmotica is unresolved and should be treated as such.**

### 2.4 Documented artefacts, and two hypotheses that were tested and rejected

Honest treatment requires separating what is established from what is assumed and from what has been ruled out.

**Established:**

- **Reduced oxygen availability.** High concentrations of PEG increase solution viscosity, limiting oxygen diffusion to the roots ✅. This imposes a hypoxic component that is not part of a water-deficit treatment. It is rarely reported or controlled for.
- **Greater tissue damage than equivalent soil drying.** In a comparison across three woody species, PEG-induced stress produced the *same* decline in water potential as soil drying but **significantly greater membrane injury and larger reductions in net photosynthesis and stomatal conductance**, with **slower recovery after stress relief** ✅. Even brief exposure was more damaging than soil drought ✅. This is among the strongest evidence that PEG and drought are not interchangeable.
- **Molecular-weight-dependent ion accumulation.** Longer exposure to PEG 400 has been associated with increased K⁺, Na⁺, Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ accumulation in the root xylem of pepper ✅ — an ionic effect in a nominally non-ionic treatment.

**Tested and rejected — state these, because listing them would be a factual error:**

- **Impurities.** Purification of commercial PEG by gel filtration did not improve maize growth relative to unpurified PEG ✅.
- **Detergent properties.** Comparison with non-ionic detergents showed detergent-like behaviour did not explain the toxicity ✅.

The mechanism Lawlor favoured instead was **physical blockage of the water-movement pathway**, reducing water absorption and desiccating the plant ✅ — a mechanical effect, not a chemical one.

**The counter-case must also be stated.** A study simulating drought in five pasture species found that under non-limiting soil contact and water-flow conditions, the equivalence of osmotic (PEG) and matric potential **held for all species**, and concluded that PEG solutions "did serve as a convenient and satisfactory media for studying the effect of true drought on seed germination" ✅. That is a genuine endorsement of PEG for exactly the application this review concerns, and it should be reported alongside the criticisms. The same study nonetheless found PEG ranked as **more severe than NaCl and more severe than mannitol** at iso-potential ✅.

### 2.5 The alternatives, and their own problems

| Agent | Principle | Documented problem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Mannitol** | Sugar alcohol, lowers water potential | Taken up into apoplast and symplast; effects partially reversible, indicating uptake ✅. Can be metabolised |
| **Sorbitol** | Sugar alcohol | Same class of problem as mannitol; metabolisable |
| **Sucrose** | Metabolite and osmoticum | Supplies carbon as well as lowering water potential; confounds stress with nutrition ✅ |
| **NaCl** | Ionic | Adds ion toxicity to water deficit — a different stress, not a substitute ✅ |
| **Solidified PEG media / raft-and-membrane systems** | Prevents PEG uptake while imposing osmotic stress | Developed for exactly this purpose in cereals ✅; less high-throughput and rarely used in Solanaceae screening |
| **Soil or vermiculite drying** | Imposes matric stress directly | Most realistic, least high-throughput; inherently more variable |

The raft and solidified-media approaches are the methodologically interesting option: they were designed to solve the penetration problem identified in §2.3, and they remain almost unused in the Solanaceae screening literature this review catalogues. That is a specific, cheap methodological gap.

### 2.6 Verdict

PEG-induced osmotic stress is fit for **relative ranking of genotypes under a defined, artificial, non-drought treatment with a stated osmoticum, concentration, temperature and duration**. It is not a mechanistic model of field drought, and three of its standard justifications — purity, non-penetration, and equivalence to drought — are either unsupported or contradicted by direct measurement.

Two recommendations follow and are carried into the proposed standard (§7):

1. **Stop calling the treatment "drought."** The most recent authoritative treatment of this question is unambiguous: PEG application triggers plant dehydration but does not accurately simulate drought, and describing such experiments as drought, dehydration or water stress **misleads readers** ✅. Terminology is not pedantry here — it determines whether a screening result is interpreted as a genotype's response to water deficit or to polyethylene glycol.
2. **Report the treatment as a chemical exposure with stated parameters**, not as a drought level.

## Reviewer-facing notes

1. **§2.3 is the section's strongest contribution.** That the "PEG does not penetrate" justification is an assumption rather than a measurement — illustrated with a direct *Capsicum* study — is a substantive methodological point, and it is the kind of thing a review should be saying. Handle it carefully: state the qualifications (slow entry; slow rates) as well as the finding.
2. **The two rejected hypotheses must stay rejected.** Impurities and detergent effects are widely repeated as artefacts. Reporting them as established would be a factual error and an easy target for a referee.
3. **Report the counter-case in §2.4.** The 1973 pasture-species study endorses PEG for germination work. Omitting it would make the section advocacy rather than review.
4. **Source quality varies.** The xylary ion accumulation and one osmometer comparison come from applied/vendor-adjacent sources ⚠️. Verify against the primary literature or attribute carefully, or drop them.
5. **The solidified-PEG-media gap is a concrete, fundable recommendation** and links §2 to §7's framework. Keep it.
6. **Do not overstate.** The defensible claim is that the treatment is under-characterised relative to how confidently it is described — not that PEG screening produces meaningless data. The 1973 equivalence result and the many successful screens stand against that stronger claim.
